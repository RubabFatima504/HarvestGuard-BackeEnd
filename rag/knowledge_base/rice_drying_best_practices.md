# Rice Drying Best Practices

This document summarizes IRRI and FAO guidance on proper rice drying — the single most influential post-harvest step for determining how long paddy can be safely stored (see `rice_storage_guide.md` for the moisture-to-shelf-life relationship this drying guidance feeds into).

## Why Drying Comes Before Everything Else

Paddy is typically harvested at 20-26% moisture content — far too wet for safe storage. At this moisture level, the grain has a high respiration rate and is highly vulnerable to mold, insects, and microbial attack. The heat generated by the grain's own respiration gets trapped inside the bulk (the husk acts as insulation), which compounds the problem. This is why drying has to happen fast: **newly harvested grain should be dried within 24 hours** to about 14% moisture for safe storage and milling, or at most 18% if only short-term (up to two weeks) storage is needed before further drying.

## Target Moisture Levels

- **14% or less** — safe for general storage of weeks to a few months (see `rice_storage_guide.md` for the full moisture/duration table).
- **13% or less** — needed for 8-12 month storage.
- **12% or less** — required if the grain is being kept as seed for the next planting.

Delayed drying risks more than just mold — it can cause non-enzymatic browning (sometimes called "stack-burning") in the grain and, particularly in parboiled rice, microbial growth and mycotoxin production. These are quality defects that occur even if the grain doesn't visibly rot, meaning a delayed-drying batch can look fine but score poorly on quality/price.

## Drying Methods

**Field drying** happens naturally as harvested paddy sits in the field before collection, and is mainly used to remove surface moisture during the rainy season or right after rain, and to reduce weight before transport. It isn't a substitute for proper drying to storage-safe moisture levels.

**Sun (solar) drying** is the cheapest and most widely used method — an estimated 70-90% of Pakistan's farm-retained paddy is dried this way. It's typically done on paved, slightly sloped areas near the warehouse or mill so that rainwater drains away, and the labor is usually performed by women and children in smallholder households. Its major weakness is that it's entirely weather-dependent and offers little control over drying speed or uniformity, which can lead to over-drying in some spots and under-drying in others within the same batch.

**Mechanical drying** offers timeliness, more uniform quality, and better control of the drying process, but requires capital investment and reliable fuel/power access — constraints that keep it out of reach for many smallholder farmers. Where available, it's generally preferred for its consistency, especially for larger volumes that would otherwise take too long to sun-dry safely.

## Temperature Control During Drying

- A drying temperature of around 43°C is recommended for seed paddy (achievable with shade drying), since higher temperatures can cause physicochemical damage to the grain that specifically affects seed viability.
- For food-grain drying using mechanical dryers, temperatures should not exceed about 54.4°C. Low drying temperatures also help preserve the natural aroma compound in rice (2-acetyl-1-pyrroline) — over-drying at high heat can measurably reduce the characteristic Basmati aroma, an important quality/price factor for this specific crop.

## Common Causes of Drying-Stage Losses

- Grain shattering from stalks or spilling from bags during transport to the drying area.
- Bird and poultry feeding on exposed drying grain.
- Over-drying, particularly common with uncontrolled sun-drying.
- Delayed drying or insufficient grain aeration, leading to stack-burning.

Published estimates put losses specifically attributable to poor drying practice at 1-5% — smaller than storage or milling losses, but almost entirely preventable with basic process discipline (timely drying, covered/paved drying areas, aeration).

## Practical Takeaways for HarvestGuard's Advisory Logic

- **Drying is the highest-leverage, lowest-cost intervention point in the entire rice post-harvest chain.** Unlike cold storage (largely inaccessible per `cold_chain_pakistan.md`), proper drying requires no special infrastructure — just timely action and a clean, covered drying surface.
- **The 24-hour drying window after harvest is the critical control point.** Advisory messaging for freshly-harvested rice batches should prioritize confirming drying has happened or is imminently planned, since this single step determines whether the batch is a "months of safe storage" case or a "weeks before spoilage" case.
- **Basmati specifically has an aroma-quality tradeoff with drying temperature** that other rice varieties may not — over-aggressive mechanical drying to save time can reduce the premium-price aroma characteristic that justifies Basmati's higher market value in the first place.
