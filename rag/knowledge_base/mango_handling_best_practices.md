# Mango Handling Best Practices

This document summarizes practical, field-level handling guidance from FAO's postharvest management manual and related industry sources, covering the period from harvest through transport to market.

## Why Handling Matters as Much as Temperature

Mango is easily bruised, and physical damage during harvesting and handling creates entry points for microorganisms that accelerate decay — independent of temperature control. A fruit that is bruised or dropped during picking will spoil faster in cold storage than an undamaged fruit at the same temperature. This means handling quality and temperature control are separate, additive levers: good handling with poor cooling still helps; good cooling can't fully compensate for rough handling.

## At Harvest

- **Harvest in the morning** when ambient temperatures are lower, to minimize the field heat the fruit picks up before any cooling can begin.
- **Handle containers gently.** Fruit should never be dropped or thrown from one container to another — every impact creates internal bruising that isn't visible until the fruit ripens and the damaged tissue breaks down faster than the rest.
- **Avoid soil contact.** Harvested mangoes placed directly on the ground pick up microbial contamination that increases disease risk during storage. Use a clean tarpaulin or container as an intermediate surface.
- **Provide shade at collection points.** Field heat can push fruit surface temperature to 35-40°C in tropical conditions (up to 45°C in extreme cases), which sharply accelerates respiration and ripening. A simple shade structure — palm fronds, mesh, or canvas — at the collection point measurably slows this heat buildup before transport begins.

## Sorting, Grading, and Packing

- **Sort out immature, damaged, and diseased fruit early**, ideally in the field or immediately at the collection point, rather than carrying defective fruit all the way to the packing stage. Damaged or diseased fruit accelerates decay in whatever it's packed alongside.
- **Grade by size and maturity.** Mixing fruit of different ripeness stages in the same container means some fruit over-ripens while waiting for the rest, and ethylene given off by more mature fruit accelerates ripening in the less mature fruit around it.
- **Minimize fruit-to-fruit contact and pressure** within containers — overpacking increases bruising risk, especially in the bottom layers of a stack.
- **Use ventilated containers** (plastic crates with airflow gaps) rather than sealed containers where possible. Poor ventilation traps heat and ethylene, both of which accelerate ripening.
- **Never use loaded containers as steps** to reach higher stacking levels — this is flagged explicitly as bad practice in FAO field guidance, since the weight and impact damages the fruit inside.

## Transport

- **Speed matters more than almost anything else after harvest.** Delays exceeding eight hours before cooling or dispatch significantly compromise quality — every hour of delay in hot conditions is effectively "spending" shelf life before the fruit even leaves the farm.
- **Cover loads to block direct sun**, but ensure the cover still allows airflow — a fully sealed tarpaulin over a stack traps heat underneath and can make things worse rather than better.
- **Avoid using the transport vehicle as general storage** for tools, equipment, or other cargo alongside the fruit — this is common informal practice but risks both physical damage and contamination.
- **Get fruit into any available cooling (shade, ventilated storage, cold storage if accessible) as soon as it arrives at the next stage**, rather than leaving it staged in the open while other logistics are arranged.

## Practical Takeaways for HarvestGuard's Advisory Logic

- **The biggest controllable loss driver before cold storage is even reached is time and heat exposure during harvest-to-transport.** A batch that sits in direct sun for several hours before being moved has already lost meaningful shelf life, regardless of what storage option is chosen afterward.
- **Handling quality is a real variable, not just temperature.** Where possible, advisory messaging should distinguish between "this batch is degrading because of storage temperature" versus "this batch may have handling damage" — the interventions are different (faster transport/shade vs. gentler harvest practices next season).
- **Morning harvest + shade at collection + fast transport is a low-cost bundle** that doesn't require cold storage access at all, and is realistic advice for farmers without cold-chain infrastructure (see `cold_chain_pakistan.md`).
