# Rice Storage Guide: Moisture, Temperature, and Duration

This document summarizes IRRI (International Rice Research Institute) guidance on safe rice storage, adapted for HarvestGuard's advisory logic on Basmati paddy.

## The Core Principle: Moisture Content Is Everything

Unlike mango, where temperature is the dominant factor in spoilage, rice storage is governed primarily by **moisture content (MC)**. Rice grain is hygroscopic — it absorbs or releases moisture depending on the humidity of the surrounding air, constantly moving toward an "equilibrium moisture content" with its environment. In Pakistan's humid, hot growing regions, this means grain left in open storage will keep absorbing moisture from the air unless it is properly dried and sealed first.

## How Long Rice Can Be Safely Stored, by Moisture Level

IRRI's guidance ties storage duration directly to how dry the grain is before it goes into storage:

| Moisture Content | Safe Storage Period | Main Risk if Exceeded |
|---|---|---|
| 14% or less | Weeks to a few months | Mold, discoloration, respiration loss, insect damage |
| 13% or less | 8-12 months | Insect damage |
| 12% or less | Suitable for farmer's seed storage | Loss of germination |
| 9% or less | Under 1 year | Loss of germination |

The pattern is straightforward: the drier the grain, the longer it can be safely held. Grain stored above 14% moisture is at serious risk of mold growth and rapid quality loss within weeks, regardless of temperature.

## The Temperature-Moisture Relationship

IRRI states a rule of thumb that is directly useful for modeling decay: **the storage life of rice is cut in half for every 1% increase in moisture content, or for every 5°C increase in storage temperature.** This means moisture and temperature act as compounding risk factors — hot, humid storage conditions are far more damaging than either factor alone. A batch of rice at borderline moisture (say, 13.5%) stored in a hot shed during Punjab's summer will spoil dramatically faster than the same batch kept cool and dry.

## Three Storage System Types

1. **Bag storage** — grain held in 40-80 kg jute or woven plastic bags. Common and low-cost, but bags do not protect against moisture re-entry unless stored in a dry, covered space.
2. **Bulk storage** — grain stored loose at the farm or at commercial collection houses. Requires good ventilation and moisture control infrastructure to be safe long-term.
3. **Hermetic (airtight) storage** — grain sealed in airtight containers (e.g., IRRI Superbag, Cocoon-type covers, or improvised local airtight containers) so the moisture content at the time of sealing stays constant. This is the most effective option for extending shelf life, since it removes the "hygroscopic drift" problem entirely. Hermetic storage also helps preserve seed germination viability and limits insect activity by starving pests of oxygen inside the sealed bag.

## Three Conditions for Safe Long-Term Storage

IRRI identifies three requirements that must all be met for rice to be stored safely over longer periods:

1. Grain moisture is at 14% or less (12% or less if it is being kept as seed).
2. Grain is protected from insects, rodents, and birds.
3. Grain is protected from re-wetting — rain exposure or moisture absorption from humid air.

Good storage hygiene reinforces all three: keeping storage areas clean and swept, treating storage rooms with insecticide between uses, using rat-traps and barriers, and inspecting stored grain weekly for signs of infestation.

## Practical Takeaways for Storage Decisions

- **Drying before storage is the single highest-leverage intervention.** A batch dried to 12-13% moisture can be stored for months; the same batch left at 15%+ moisture may spoil within weeks.
- **Temperature and moisture compound each other.** Hot storage sheds are especially risky for rice that hasn't been dried to a safe moisture level — the "halves every 5°C" rule means summer heat in Punjab can turn a marginal batch into a spoiled one quickly.
- **Hermetic storage is the best available option for extending shelf life** when cold storage infrastructure isn't available, since rice (unlike mango) doesn't require refrigeration to be preserved effectively — it requires moisture control.
- **Rice is far more forgiving over time than mango when properly dried.** Where mango's storage window is measured in days to weeks, correctly dried and sealed rice can be safely held for months to over a year, which changes the urgency calculus completely compared to fresh fruit.
