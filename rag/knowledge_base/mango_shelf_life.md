# Mango Shelf-Life: Sindhri and Chaunsa

This document summarizes published postharvest research on two of Pakistan's major commercial mango cultivars — Sindhri and Chaunsa (including its Samar Bahisht and Sufaid variants) — to guide storage and transport decisions for HarvestGuard.

## Why These Two Cultivars Matter

Sindhri and Samar Bahisht Chaunsa are Pakistan's leading export cultivars, together with Sufaid Chaunsa. Both are climacteric fruits, meaning they continue to ripen and soften after harvest, and both are highly perishable at ambient subtropical temperatures. Export quality depends heavily on how quickly the fruit is cooled and how well temperature is controlled through the supply chain.

## Sindhri: Storage Behavior

Research on Sindhri (alongside Sufaid Chaunsa) storage at 11°C and 80-85% relative humidity found that fruit quality holds up reasonably well through the first two to three weeks of cold storage. Marketable fruit percentage stayed close to 79% after two weeks, dropping to roughly 71% by the third week and around 60% by the fourth week. This decline is driven mainly by disease development and textural softening rather than any single failure point — the fruit degrades gradually rather than collapsing suddenly.

Compared to Chaunsa, Sindhri consistently showed better disease resistance and a more predictable decline curve, which is part of why it remains Pakistan's most exported mango variety. Under normal (non-modified-atmosphere) cold storage, published literature puts the practical storage ceiling for mango generally at around three weeks before quality becomes commercially unacceptable.

## Chaunsa: Storage Behavior

Chaunsa is noticeably more sensitive to storage duration and temperature than Sindhri, and its two main variants behave somewhat differently.

**Samar Bahisht Chaunsa**, stored at a slightly lower temperature (around 13°C, 85-90% RH) using modified atmosphere packaging (MAP), reached its best quality at 14 days — around 92% of fruit remained marketable at that point. Past 21 days, quality fell off sharply: by day 21 only about 38% of fruit was still marketable, and by day 28-35 that number dropped to roughly 11%. Chaunsa is also particularly prone to chilling injury (a form of cold-storage damage marked by skin discoloration and uneven ripening) when kept in ordinary cold storage for extended periods, which is one reason MAP bagging technology was tested for this variety specifically.

**Sufaid Chaunsa**, stored at 11°C and 80-85% RH, followed a somewhat gentler decline: about 90% marketable at two weeks, 83% at three weeks, 67% at four weeks, and 43% by five weeks. However, this variant showed higher disease incidence overall than Sindhri, meaning careful fungicidal treatment and hygiene during handling matter more for Chaunsa shipments.

## Practical Takeaways for Storage Decisions

- **Cold storage (10-14°C, 80-90% RH) roughly doubles or triples usable shelf life** compared to ambient tropical conditions, where both cultivars typically hold for only a few days before becoming unmarketable.
- **The first two weeks of storage matter most.** Both cultivars retain the great majority of their value if moved into cold storage and sold or shipped within 14 days. Waiting past three weeks sharply increases the risk of unsellable stock, especially for Chaunsa.
- **Chaunsa is the more time-sensitive of the two crops.** If a shipment must be delayed or a market decision deferred, Sindhri has more buffer than Chaunsa before losses become severe.
- **MAP bagging technology (elevated CO2, reduced O2) can extend Chaunsa's window** by suppressing ripening and disease development, though it requires proper packaging materials that may not be available to all farmers/cooperatives.
- **Disease control (fungicidal dip, hot water treatment before storage) is as important as temperature control.** A meaningful share of quality loss in both cultivars comes from disease and discoloration, not just natural ripening.
